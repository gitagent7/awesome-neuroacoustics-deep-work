## Read this first

* Binaural-beat research is **mixed**. Studies differ in method, sample, and result, and a systematic review is the best place to start ([Ingendoh et al., 2023](#binaural-beats-overview)).
* The famous 40 Hz studies from MIT used **flickering light, and light plus sound, in mice**. They did not test binaural beats and did not test people.
* Nothing here is medical advice. SYNAPSE // NEURAL AMBIENT sessions are listening sessions for studying, working, and sleeping. They do not treat insomnia, ADHD, memory disease, or any other condition.
* Each entry below says what the study **did** and **did not** test. If a sentence here goes beyond its paper, [open an issue](../../issues) and it will be narrowed.

## How a binaural beat works

Play two steady tones, one in each ear. Their difference is the beat.

```
left ear  = carrier - (beat / 2)
right ear = carrier + (beat / 2)

Example, 40 Hz beat on a 216 Hz carrier:   196 Hz left, 236 Hz right
```

* **Headphones are required.** The beat is only a *binaural* beat if each ear gets its own tone.
* **On speakers** the two tones mix in the air before they reach you. You may hear a pulse in the room, but it is an ordinary acoustic beat, not a binaural one.
* Oster (1973), listed below, is the classic popular account of the effect.

## Reading list

Format: authors (year), title, journal, DOI, then a note on what the study did and did not test.

### Binaural beats: overview

* Ingendoh, R. M., Posny, E. S., & Heine, A. (2023). *Binaural beats to entrain the brain? A systematic review of the effects of binaural beat stimulation on brain oscillatory activity, and the implications for psychological research and intervention.* PLOS ONE, 18(5), e0286023. [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0286023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286023)
  *Systematic review. Start here: it looks at how consistently binaural beats change brain oscillatory activity across studies.*
* Oster, G. (1973). *Auditory beats in the brain.* Scientific American, 229(4), 94-102. [doi:10.1038/scientificamerican1073-94](https://doi.org/10.1038/scientificamerican1073-94)
  *Classic popular account that introduced binaural beats to a wide audience. Dated; read it for the history.*
* Lane, J. D., Kasian, S. J., Owens, J. E., & Marsh, G. R. (1998). *Binaural auditory beats affect vigilance performance and mood.* Physiology & Behavior, 63(2), 249-252. [doi:10.1016/S0031-9384(97)00436-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0031-9384(97)00436-8)
  *Compared beats in the beta range with beats in the theta and delta ranges on a vigilance task and a mood measure. Did not test 10 Hz or 40 Hz.*
* Colzato, L. S., Barone, H., Sellaro, R., & Hommel, B. (2017). *More attentional focusing through binaural beats: evidence from the global-local task.* Psychological Research, 81(1), 271-277. [doi:10.1007/s00426-015-0727-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-015-0727-0)
  *One lab experiment comparing binaural beats with a control condition on an attention task. A single study; replication matters.*

### 40 Hz gamma

* Ross, B., Herdman, A. T., & Pantev, C. (2005). *Right hemispheric laterality of human 40 Hz auditory steady-state responses.* Cerebral Cortex, 15(12), 2029-2039. [doi:10.1093/cercor/bhi078](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhi078)
  *Measured 40 Hz auditory steady-state responses to modulated sound and compared the two hemispheres. The stimulus was modulated sound, not a binaural pair.*
* Iaccarino, H. F., Singer, A. C., Martorell, A. J., et al. (2016). *Gamma frequency entrainment attenuates amyloid load and modifies microglia.* Nature, 540(7632), 230-235. [doi:10.1038/nature20587](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature20587)
  *Used 40 Hz flickering light in mouse models of Alzheimer's disease. Not sound, not binaural beats, not tested in people.*
* Martorell, A. J., Paulson, A. L., Suk, H.-J., et al. (2019). *Multi-sensory gamma stimulation ameliorates Alzheimer's-associated pathology and improves cognition.* Cell, 177(2), 256-271. [doi:10.1016/j.cell.2019.02.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2019.02.014)
  *40 Hz flickering light in mouse models of Alzheimer's disease (sound was added in the 2019 follow-up). Not binaural beats, not tested in people.*
* Ross, B., & Lopez, M. D. (2020). *40-Hz binaural beats enhance training to mitigate the attentional blink.* Scientific Reports, 10(1). [doi:10.1038/s41598-020-63980-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-63980-y)
  *A training study reporting an effect of 40 Hz binaural beats on one attention task. A narrow task and a single study.*
* Başar, E. (2013). *A review of gamma oscillations in healthy subjects and in cognitive impairment.* International Journal of Psychophysiology, 90(2), 99-117. [doi:10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2013.07.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2013.07.005)
  *Review of what gamma rhythms are and how they behave in health and in impairment. Background; no listening intervention.*

### 10 Hz alpha

* Klimesch, W. (1999). *EEG alpha and theta oscillations reflect cognitive and memory performance: a review and analysis.* Brain Research Reviews, 29(2-3), 169-195. [doi:10.1016/S0165-0173(98)00056-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0165-0173(98)00056-3)
  *Review linking alpha and theta rhythms to cognitive and memory performance. About the brain's own rhythms, not about sound.*
* Jensen, O., & Mazaheri, A. (2010). *Shaping functional architecture by oscillatory alpha activity: gating by inhibition.* Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 4, 186. [doi:10.3389/fnhum.2010.00186](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2010.00186)
  *Proposes that alpha activity reduces processing in regions not needed for the task. A theory paper; no listening intervention.*

### 14 Hz sensorimotor rhythm

* Sterman, M. B. (1996). *Physiological origins and functional correlates of EEG rhythmic activities: implications for self-regulation.* Biofeedback and Self-Regulation, 21(1), 3-33. [doi:10.1007/BF02214147](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02214147)
  *Review of the sensorimotor rhythm and its link to self-regulation. Neurofeedback background, not sound.*
* Egner, T., & Gruzelier, J. H. (2004). *EEG biofeedback of low beta band components: frequency-specific effects on variables of attention and event-related brain potentials.* Clinical Neurophysiology, 115(1), 131-139. [doi:10.1016/S1388-2457(03)00353-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1388-2457(03)00353-5)
  *Healthy adults trained their own brain activity with feedback. They were not listening to sound.*

### 1.5 Hz delta and sleep

* Marshall, L., Helgadóttir, H., Mölle, M., & Born, J. (2006). *Boosting slow oscillations during sleep potentiates memory.* Nature, 444(7119), 610-613. [doi:10.1038/nature05278](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05278)
  *Applied weak electrical stimulation at slow-oscillation frequency during sleep and reported better memory retention. Electrical, not sound.*
* Ngo, H.-V. V., Martinetz, T., Born, J., & Mölle, M. (2013). *Auditory closed-loop stimulation of the sleep slow oscillation enhances memory.* Neuron, 78(3), 545-553. [doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2013.03.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.03.006)
  *Played short bursts of sound timed to each sleeper's own slow oscillations. Timed bursts, not a continuous tone.*
* Papalambros, N. A., Santostasi, G., Malkani, R. G., et al. (2017). *Acoustic enhancement of sleep slow oscillations and concomitant memory improvement in older adults.* Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 11, 109. [doi:10.3389/fnhum.2017.00109](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00109)
  *Timed sound during sleep in older adults. Timed to the sleeper's own rhythm, not a fixed beat.*
* Bellesi, M., Riedner, B. A., Garcia-Molina, G. N., Cirelli, C., & Tononi, G. (2014). *Enhancement of sleep slow waves: underlying mechanisms and practical consequences.* Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience, 8, 208. [doi:10.3389/fnsys.2014.00208](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2014.00208)
  *Review of ways to enhance sleep slow waves, including sound. A review, not a test of any file.*
* Tononi, G., & Cirelli, C. (2014). *Sleep and the price of plasticity: from synaptic and cellular homeostasis to memory consolidation and integration.* Neuron, 81(1), 12-34. [doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2013.12.025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.12.025)
  *Review of why sleep matters for the brain. Background; no listening intervention.*

### Noise and sound masking

* Söderlund, G., Sikström, S., & Smart, A. (2007). *Listen to the noise: noise is beneficial for cognitive performance in ADHD.* Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 48(8), 840-847. [doi:10.1111/j.1469-7610.2007.01749.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2007.01749.x)
  *Reported that background noise improved task performance in children with ADHD. A lab task, not a long listening session.*
* Stanchina, M. L., Abu-Hijleh, M., Chaudhry, B. K., Carlisle, C. C., & Millman, R. P. (2005). *The influence of white noise on sleep in subjects exposed to ICU noise.* Sleep Medicine, 6(5), 423-428. [doi:10.1016/j.sleep.2004.12.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2004.12.004)
  *Healthy volunteers slept with recorded hospital noise, with and without white noise. Not brown noise, and not a test of any file.*

## Listening guide

1. **Use stereo headphones for binaural sessions.** On speakers the two tones mix in the air and the binaural effect is lost. Sessions are still comfortable to play on a bedside speaker.
2. **Keep the volume low, about 30-40%.** The sound should sit under your work, not compete with it.
3. **One task per block.** Pick the task before you press play.
4. **For sleep, dim the screen or turn it face down.** The sleep sessions fade to a true black screen after 30 minutes.
5. **Stop if anything feels uncomfortable.**
